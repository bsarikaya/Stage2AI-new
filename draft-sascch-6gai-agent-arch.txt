3.  Operating AI Agents Safely

   The solution variants (SVs) for Key Issue (KI) #18 in [TR23.801-01]
   let an AI agent turn an intent into actions on network functions
   (NFs).  This section looks at what an operator needs before allowing
   that on a live network: a bound on what the agent may change, a check
   before a change is applied, a way to undo a change and to stop the
   agent, and a record of who changed what.  For each of the four, it
   describes where 3GPP stands, what is still open, and which IETF work
   can be reused.

3.1.  Status in 3GPP and Approach

   At the time of writing, the only interim agreement on operator
   control under KI#18 is clause 7.1.18.1 of [TR23.801-01].  It states
   that the 6G core network supports authorising or controlling the
   sending of intent requests per requested service or per User
   Equipment (UE) or Application Function (AF), and supports activating
   and deactivating AI capabilities per NF.  Whether and how the
   operator controls AI observability and traceability is for further
   study, and the details of control through operations, administration
   and maintenance (OAM) are left to 3GPP SA WG5.  Everything else cited
   below from clause 6.18 is a proposal in an SV, not an agreement.
   Agreed text for KI#19 (clauses 7.1.19.1 and 7.1.19.2) is cited where
   it is relevant and is marked as KI#19.

   The choice between NW-Agent functions and an ISF (clause 6.18.1),
   intent handling embedded in existing NFs, with or without AI (clause
   6.18.2), and a separate AI domain (clause 6.18.3) is also open.  This
   section therefore uses three role names that do not depend on that
   choice:

   agent:  The entity that plans and invokes actions to fulfil an
      intent, for example the NW-Agent functions or the intent
      fulfilment logic of the ISF.  The term names a role.  An NW-Agent
      function can play it, but the two terms are not the same.

   enforcement point:  An entity under operator control that decides
      whether an action proposed by an agent is applied.  Examples are
      the intermediary that SV#18.1 places under operator control in the
      NW-Agent option, the gateway function of the ISF, and the target
      itself.

   target:  The NF, or the management service of an NF, on which an
      action is performed.

   Actions reach a target on two paths: at run time, as service
   operations over the service-based interface or as tool invocations
   over a tool-based interface, and through the management plane, as
   configuration changes made through OAM.  The requirements below apply
   to both.  Several of the IETF mechanisms cited come from NETCONF and
   RESTCONF.  They are cited to show that a property is already
   specified for network management, not to suggest that 3GPP NFs are
   configured with these protocols.  Whether and how any of them is used
   with 3GPP interfaces is for the groups concerned to decide.

   None of these requirements is specific to AI.  They apply to any
   automation that changes a network.  They become necessary with agents
   because the actions are selected at run time instead of being written
   and reviewed in advance, and because an agent can act faster than a
   person can review.  Where [I-D.mcw-opsawg-icon-requirements] or
   [I-D.ietf-wimse-aims] already states a requirement, it is repeated
   here only to relate it to the 3GPP entities and to the 3GPP status.
   The analysis of the gap in 3GPP is what this section adds.

   The requirements are numbered for discussion.  The key words in them
   describe what an operator needs from a solution, whether it is built
   from 3GPP procedures, IETF protocols or both.  They do not place
   conformance obligations on implementations of this document.

3.2.  Scope of Authority

   In the 5G service-based architecture, access to NF services is
   authorised with OAuth 2.0 access tokens issued by the Network
   Repository Function (NRF) (clause 13.4.1 of [TS33.501]).  A token
   names the consumer NF instance, the producer NF type or producer NF
   instances, the services, optionally the operations and resources,
   optionally a list of slices or NF sets, and an expiry time.  The
   claims defined in that clause do not include the task or intent being
   served, the UEs or parameter values affected, or the size of a
   change.  Annex X of [TS33.501] adds a claim that names the source NF
   when an intermediary NF acts on its behalf, but only for the network
   automation services of that annex, such as access to data, analytics
   and AI/ML models.  Token revocation is not supported.

   For agents, SV#18.22 proposes a boundary of allowed NFs, NF services,
   slices, UEs and services that OAM configures statically (clause
   6.18.22.1.1.1 of [TR23.801-01]), and autonomy levels that can be
   raised or lowered (clause 6.18.22.0).  Neither the boundary nor the
   autonomy levels are agreed, and the SV itself notes that
   configuration through OAM cannot guarantee control in real time.  The
   agreed UE AI agent identifier (KI#19, clause 7.1.19.1) applies to
   operator-controlled agents on UEs.  No agreed text defines an
   identifier or a credential for an agent in the network.  Some SVs
   propose that agents register with an agent identifier (for example
   clause 6.18.17.0), but they do not say how it is authenticated or
   tied to authority.  [TR33.801-01] has no key issue dedicated to
   KI#18.  Its key issue on agentic exposure (clause 5.6.3.3) covers the
   exposure aspects of KI#18 and KI#19, and in its clause on UE AI agent
   security (clause 5.8.1) the aspects related to KI#18 are marked for
   further study.

   The agreed controls decide who may send an intent and whether an NF
   may use AI.  They do not state what a particular agent may change
   while it fulfils a particular intent.

   AUTH-1:  Authority MUST be given to an agent as an explicit grant
      issued by a function under operator control.  An agent MUST NOT be
      able to issue or widen its own grant.

   AUTH-2:  A grant MUST identify the agent and the intent or task it
      serves, and MUST list the permitted operations and targets, for
      example NF instances, slices and UE groups.  It SHOULD be able to
      carry limits on the size and rate of change.  Anything that is not
      listed is not permitted.

   AUTH-3:  A grant MUST expire.  Its lifetime SHOULD follow the task,
      not a general token lifetime set for the whole network.

   AUTH-4:  The operator MUST be able to revoke a grant before it
      expires.  Operator policy sets an upper bound on the time until a
      revocation takes effect at the enforcement point, and the operator
      MUST be able to observe that the bound is met.

   AUTH-5:  When an agent delegates part of a task to another agent, the
      derived grant MUST NOT exceed the original in any respect, and the
      chain of delegation MUST be recorded.

   AUTH-6:  Grants MUST be checked at an enforcement point that the
      agent cannot modify or bypass.  A request outside the grant MUST
      be refused, and the refusal MUST be reported to the operator.

   Several related IETF specifications exist.  [I-D.ietf-wimse-aims]
   gives each agent a unique identifier, uses OAuth for delegation,
   states that the scope of a token obtained by exchange never exceeds
   that of the token presented, and relies on short-lived credentials
   and on revocation and risk signals.  Transaction tokens
   [I-D.ietf-oauth-transaction-tokens] bind authorisation context to a
   single transaction within a trust domain.  Rich Authorization
   Requests [RFC9396] can carry typed, structured permissions; a type
   that expresses slices, NF instances, UE groups and numeric limits
   would have to be defined, and the authors are not aware of one.
   Token exchange [RFC8693] records the acting party and earlier actors
   in the "act" claim.  For configuration data, the NETCONF Access
   Control Model (NACM) [RFC8341] shows enforcement at the managed
   server down to individual data nodes, but its rules are per user or
   group and have no notion of task, lifetime or delegation.  A grant
   also needs a stable identifier for the agent.  DNS-based agent naming
   such as [I-D.cui-dns-native-agent-naming-resolution] is one way to
   provide a name that stays the same across versions of an agent.
   [I-D.mcw-opsawg-icon-requirements] has requirements on restricting
   the scope of a network management agent to given domains, devices,
   protocols and tools, and on raising and lowering its level of
   authorisation.  It does not require a grant that is tied to a task,
   that expires, or that cannot be widened on delegation.

3.3.  Pre-Action Verification

   SV#18.22 proposes that operator control may evaluate selected AI-
   derived candidate actions, namely those that change network state or
   service behaviour that is visible from outside, before they are
   enforced, where operator policy so provides.  The evaluation has five
   possible dispositions: it can allow the action, allow it within a
   narrower scope, ask for more explanation or context, redirect it to a
   defined or default procedure, or deny it (clause 6.18.22.0 of
   [TR23.801-01]).  A note adds that the evaluation of an explanation
   does not authorise an action that is prohibited on other grounds,
   such as operator policy or security requirements.  SV#18.1 describes
   an intermediary under operator control that verifies intents, plans
   and candidate actions before enforcement (clause 6.18.1.1.1.1).
   SV#18.6 has an optional step in which, if digital twin simulation is
   supported and enabled by operator policy, the executing function may
   invoke the digital twin for validation before the live invocation
   (clause 6.18.6.2.5); the text does not say what is validated or how
   the result is used.  None of this is agreed.  In the ISF option, the
   gateway function is listed both with translating invocation requests
   into service requests and with authorising, auditing and verifying
   intent actions; whether it is deployed separately is for further
   study (clause 6.18.1.1.1.2).  The intermediary of SV#18.1 may
   likewise be co-located with the NW-Agent function.  If the gateway
   function or the intermediary is also part of the agent, it cannot
   satisfy VER-1 by itself.

   Two precedents exist on the management side.  In coordination of
   self-organising network (SON) functions (clause 4.7.4.2 of
   [TS28.628]), a SON function can request permission from a
   coordination function before it changes certain parameters.  The
   coordination function typically bases its answer on inputs such as
   the current and the proposed values and the likely impact on other
   objects and on performance indicators.  Intent-driven management
   (clause 5.3.3 of [TS28.312]) has a feasibility check of an intent
   before fulfilment, which can use network digital twin functions to
   simulate the resulting management tasks.  It does not define a check
   that is applied to each configuration change made while the intent is
   fulfilled.

   The proposed evaluation is defined mainly by its results.  The input
   it may consider is listed only at a high level (clause 6.18.22.1.1.0
   of [TR23.801-01]): the action and its category, the affected scope
   and expected impact, explainability-related information, confidence,
   operator policy and validity information.  The details are for
   further study.  The list does not include whether the state the agent
   acted on is still current, whether the action can be undone, or what
   the state was before.

   VER-1:  The check MUST be performed by an entity other than the agent
      that proposes the action.  Its result MUST NOT rest only on
      information supplied by that agent.

   VER-2:  A request to change a target MUST identify the version of the
      target's state on which the agent based its decision.  The change
      MUST be refused if the part of that state on which the decision
      relied has changed since.

   VER-3:  The check MUST detect other changes that are pending or in
      progress on the same target, whether they come from another agent,
      an automated control loop or a person.

   VER-4:  Each action MUST be marked as reversible or not reversible.
      An action that cannot be undone MUST require a stricter level of
      approval, set by operator policy.

   VER-5:  Before a change is applied, the state of each target MUST be
      recorded.

   VER-6:  A request MUST state its expected effect as one or more
      observable measurements with thresholds.

   VER-7:  The check MUST return one of a closed, documented set of
      results and MUST record the information on which the result was
      based.  A request that fails VER-2 MUST be refused.

   The recorded state (VER-5) and the expected effect (VER-6) are used
   later for rollback and for attribution.  The five dispositions
   proposed in SV#18.22 are one candidate for the set of results in VER-
   7.

   The IETF and the IRTF have parts of this for configuration
   management.  RESTCONF (Section 3.4.1.2 of [RFC8040]) lets a client
   send the entity-tag it received when it read configuration data in an
   "If-Match" header, and the server rejects the edit if the tag no
   longer matches.  The entity-tag reflects configuration data only, not
   operational state.  The NETCONF validate capability (Section 8.6 of
   [RFC6241]) checks a proposed configuration at the server without
   applying it.  The Network Management Datastore Architecture (NMDA)
   [RFC8342] separates intended from operational state.  Datastore
   comparison [RFC9144] reports the differences between two datastores,
   which can show whether a change has taken effect; it does not record
   the state before a change.  [I-D.irtf-nmrg-network-digital-twin-arch]
   describes, as a use case, verifying the impact of a change in a
   network digital twin before it is applied to the production network.
   None of these defines the check as a whole, and validating a
   configuration against its data model is not a check of its effect on
   the network.

3.4.  Rollback and Containment

   SV#18.22 proposes, for some of the architecture options, that the
   network can always switch to default procedures that do not use AI,
   in a forced or a graceful way, that default handling may include a
   return to a previously known safe state, and that an intermediary can
   stop forwarding tool invocations from agents (clauses 6.18.22.0 and
   6.18.22.1.1.2.3 of [TR23.801-01]).  Whether both forced and graceful
   switching are needed, and how service continuity is kept during the
   switch, are open.  SV#18.15 has a participating NF stop or revert an
   exploratory action of reinforcement learning when a safeguard in the
   exploration policy is breached (clause 6.18.15.2.1.2).  For KI#18,
   the agreed text provides only activation and deactivation of AI
   capabilities per NF (clause 7.1.18.1).  There is no agreed text on
   stopping an agent or on undoing its changes.  For KI#19, the network
   and the consumer can terminate an AI training or inference service
   (clause 7.1.19.2), which is termination of a service and not the
   reversal of actions on NFs.

   Switching AI off for future requests is different from undoing what
   an agent has already done.  A plan that fulfils one intent can change
   several NFs.  SV#18.22 mentions a return to the last known
   configuration of an NF, but if a plan fails or is stopped halfway,
   the SVs do not say how the completed steps of a plan that spans
   several NFs are identified and reversed.

   RBK-1:  A plan that changes more than one target MUST have one
      identifier, carried in every change made under it, so that the
      changes can be found and reversed as a unit.

   RBK-2:  Reverting a plan, in whole or from a given step, MUST be
      possible without the cooperation of the agent that created it.
      The order of reversal MUST be defined.

   RBK-3:  A change SHOULD be provisional until it is confirmed.  A
      provisional change MUST be reverted without waiting for the agent
      if the expected effect (VER-6) is not observed, or a health
      condition defined by the operator is breached, within a time
      defined by the operator.  Actions marked as not reversible (VER-4)
      are outside this requirement.

   RBK-4:  The operator MUST be able to suspend an agent and revoke its
      grants (AUTH-4) on a path that does not pass through the agent.
      The response MUST distinguish a stop that has been accepted from
      one that has taken effect.

   RBK-5:  Operator policy MUST be able to limit, per plan and per
      period of time, how many targets, slices and UEs a plan may
      affect.

   RBK-6:  The specification of each kind of action MUST state what
      happens to established sessions when the action is reverted or the
      agent is stopped.

   NETCONF confirmed commit (Section 8.4 of [RFC6241]) is the closest
   existing model.  A change is reverted automatically unless it is
   confirmed within a timeout, and with a persist identifier a session
   other than the one that made the change can confirm or cancel it.
   Confirmed commit applies to one device only.  Appendix E.2 of
   [RFC6241], which is not normative, describes how a manager can
   coordinate changes on several devices by locking, validating, taking
   checkpoints and restoring them on failure.  The authors are not aware
   of an IETF standard for a confirmed commit across several devices, so
   the entity that coordinates the plan has to provide RBK-1 to RBK-3.
   [I-D.mcw-opsawg-icon-requirements] has requirements on interrupting
   an agent, including a stop that does not depend on the agent and the
   distinction between accepted and applied, and on rollback.  It does
   not define an identifier, a protocol or a mapping to 3GPP entities.

3.5.  Attribution

   SV#18.6 proposes a correlation identifier for each intent being
   fulfilled.  It is associated with the fulfilment plan and may be used
   by the participating entities to tie their actions and events
   together.  The actions, the entities involved, the configuration
   values and the outcomes are stored as traceability information
   (clause 6.18.6.1.2.3 of [TR23.801-01]).  The Intent Correlation ID of
   clause 6.18.7 identifies a set of related intents of one requester,
   and whether it is needed is for further study.  The TR uses similar
   names for the two identifiers, so the relation between them is not
   settled either.  In SV#18.22, entities record their own reasoning and
   decision history.  In SV#18.1, the access control intermediary
   produces audit logs for monitoring at request level and sends them to
   an Audit Function that clause 6.18.1 does not define.  SV#18.19
   proposes monitoring in which a monitoring function may also check
   whether execution matched the request; whether this applies is for
   further study.  In the management study, a potential solution for the
   observability of management agents (clause 9.3.1.4.2.1 of
   [TR32.801-01]) has the management agent report its own operation log,
   including who invoked the operation, to a consumer that subscribed to
   it.  As noted in Section 3.1, operator control of observability and
   traceability is not yet agreed.

   Several of these proposals have the entity that acted record its own
   history.  Others store or collect records in a separate registry or
   monitoring function.  None requires that the records are kept
   independently of the acting entity, that they are protected against
   alteration or loss, or that they carry a time reference with a stated
   accuracy.  Agent identifiers exist for registration and discovery.
   SV#18.6 lists the agent involved among its traceability information,
   and SV#18.22 says that traceability information may correlate an
   action with the decision of operator control and with the result.  No
   proposal states that the record identifies the grant of the
   requesting agent, or the chain of delegation under which the change
   was made.

   ATT-1:  Each change made for an agent MUST carry one or more
      identifiers that together allow the agent, its grant, the intent
      and the plan to be retrieved.

   ATT-2:  The enforcement point MUST produce its own record of each
      change.  The agent's record can explain why a change was made.  It
      MUST NOT be the only evidence of what was changed.

   ATT-3:  The record MUST show the requester of the intent and every
      agent in the chain of delegation, not only the identity used on
      the last hop.

   ATT-4:  A party other than the producer of a record MUST be able to
      detect that the record has been altered, removed or cut short.

   ATT-5:  Records MUST carry a time from a known source with a stated
      accuracy, so that a change can be related to performance
      measurements taken before and after it.

   ATT-6:  Identifiers used for correlation SHOULD NOT contain
      subscriber identifiers.

   ATT-7:  The operator MUST be able to set the retention period of
      records and the rules for access to them.

   NETCONF configuration change notifications [RFC6470] are produced by
   the server.  They identify either the server itself or the user name,
   session and source host of the management session that made a change,
   but they have no field for the party on whose behalf it was made.
   Trace context propagation for NETCONF
   [I-D.ietf-netconf-trace-ctx-extension] lets a client attach a W3C
   trace context to a request, and lets the server add a trace context
   to the reply and to notifications.  It correlates records; it does
   not authenticate the party that set the value.  YANG data provenance
   [I-D.ietf-opsawg-yang-provenance] lets the producer of YANG data sign
   it, and later parties can add countersignatures.  The signatures
   cover the origin and integrity of the data; nothing in them says who
   asked for the change.  The SCITT architecture [RFC9943] provides
   append-only logs of signed statements with receipts, which could
   serve ATT-4.  The architecture in
   [I-D.kuehlewind-audit-architecture], written for the proposed AUDIT
   work in the IETF, has the services record what they observe at their
   own boundary.  It states that an agent that records itself cannot by
   itself deliver non-repudiation to a third party, which is consistent
   with ATT-2.  [I-D.ietf-wimse-aims] lists the minimum content of an
   audit event for authorisation decisions and requires audit records to
   be tamper-evident.  [I-D.mcw-opsawg-icon-requirements] has
   requirements on accountability, tamper-evident telemetry and an
   authoritative clock source.
